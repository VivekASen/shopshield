Helpful information on shopping addiction

If shopping or spending is causing distress, financial harm, or interfering with daily life, professional help and self-help resources can make a big difference. Below are reputable resources to learn more and find support.

Quick takeaways
  • Compulsive buying (shopping addiction, oniomania) can cause distress and financial harm; therapy—especially cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)—is among the evidence-based treatments. (See reviews below.)
  • Support groups, financial counseling, and treatment for co-occurring mental health issues are commonly recommended alongside therapy.

Resources

  • Healthline — Shopping addiction (overview)
    Overview of symptoms, triggers, and treatments (CBT, support groups, professional help). Updated article with medical review and practical guidance.
  • Black DW — A review of compulsive buying disorder (2007) — PMC
    Academic review covering clinical features, prevalence, and early evidence about treatment approaches. Good for understanding clinical context and research history.
  • Vasiliu et al. — Therapeutic management of buying/shopping disorder (2022) — PMC
    Recent review summarizing psychotherapeutic approaches (CBT), and considerations for treating buying/shopping disorder. Useful for evidence-based treatment options.
  • SAMHSA — National behavioral health resources
    US federal behavioral health resource hub. Use the site to find local treatment resources, crisis lines, and guidance for finding care.
  • Verywell Mind — Self-help groups & resources
    Practical suggestions about peer support groups (e.g., Debtors Anonymous, Spending Addicts groups) and self-help programs that many people find helpful.
  • NHS — Mental health conditions (UK)
    General UK NHS mental health information; useful if you want trusted health guidance and links to local NHS services (if in the UK).
Not a crisis resource: if someone is in immediate danger or a mental health emergency, call your local emergency number or crisis line (e.g., 988 in the U.S.).
Sources: Healthline; NHS; SAMHSA; peer-reviewed reviews (PMC). See links above.
